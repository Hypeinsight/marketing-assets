## Is My Website Hurting My Business? (Start Here)

Before diving into the full diagnostic, take two minutes to answer the following questions honestly. Circle or note your answers.

1. Have you ever sent a potential client to your website and felt slightly embarrassed by it?
2. Do you receive more enquiries from word of mouth than from your website?
3. Has a client ever told you they could not find your contact details easily?
4. Does your website look noticeably older than your competitors' sites?
5. Have you ever lost a quote or proposal because a prospect "checked out your website" and went elsewhere?
6. Do you avoid mentioning your website in conversations because you know it does not reflect your business well?
7. Has it been more than 12 months since you reviewed your website's performance?
8. Do you have no idea how many people visit your website each month?
9. Does your website load slowly, even on your own device?
10. Are you unsure whether your website is secure or up to date?

**If you answered yes to 3 or more questions**, your website is likely costing you business. This guide will help you understand exactly where the problems are — and what to do about them.

**If you answered yes to 6 or more questions**, a professional website review is strongly recommended. The diagnostic tools in this guide will help you prepare for that conversation.

## Before You Begin

This checklist is designed to be completed in a single sitting of approximately 60 to 90 minutes. No technical background is required. Every item is written in plain English, and where a tool is needed to test something, the tool is named and explained.

As you work through each item, mark it as one of the following:

- **Pass** — This is working well on your website.
- **Partial** — This exists but could be improved.
- **Fail** — This is missing or broken on your website.

At the end, you will tally your results using the Revenue Leak Scorecard to understand the severity of your leaks and where to focus first.

This checklist is best suited to business owners who built their own website, those who inherited a site from a previous developer, or those who have not reviewed their website in over 12 months. If you are about to invest in a new website, use this checklist to evaluate your current site before briefing a developer — it will make that conversation significantly more productive.

## Chapter 1 — The Cost of Inaction: What Revenue Leaks Actually Cost You

Most business owners think about their website in terms of what it cost to build. Very few think about what it costs them every month it underperforms. This chapter reframes the conversation.

Consider a straightforward example. Your website receives 500 visitors per month. Your current conversion rate — the percentage of visitors who make an enquiry — is 1%. That means you are generating five leads per month from your website. Now consider what happens if fixing the leaks identified in this checklist improves your conversion rate to just 2%. You are now generating ten leads per month — double the result — without spending an additional dollar on advertising, social media, or SEO.

The table below illustrates the compounding effect of small conversion improvements across different traffic volumes.

| Monthly Visitors | 1% Conversion Rate | 2% Conversion Rate | 3% Conversion Rate |
|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 500 | 5 leads | 10 leads | 15 leads |
| 1,000 | 10 leads | 20 leads | 30 leads |
| 2,500 | 25 leads | 50 leads | 75 leads |
| 5,000 | 50 leads | 100 leads | 150 leads |

Before you continue, answer this question: **What is one additional lead worth to your business?** For a trades business, a single additional job might be worth $800 to $3,000. For a professional services firm, a new client relationship might be worth $5,000 to $50,000 over its lifetime. Multiply that value by the additional leads a 1% conversion improvement would generate at your current traffic level. That number is the cost of leaving your website's revenue leaks unaddressed.

## Chapter 2 — How Websites Lose Revenue

Before running the checklist, it helps to understand the categories of revenue leaks. Most website problems fall into one of six categories, and understanding the category helps you prioritise fixes.

**Messaging leaks** occur when visitors cannot quickly understand what the business does, who it serves, or why they should choose it over a competitor. A visitor who is confused leaves. The fix is almost always in the words on the page, not the design.

**Speed leaks** occur when a website loads too slowly. Research consistently shows that visitors abandon pages that take more than three seconds to load. Speed leaks are particularly damaging on mobile devices, where the majority of web traffic now originates.

**UX (User Experience) leaks** occur when visitors cannot easily navigate the site, find what they are looking for, or take the action they intended. Poor UX is often invisible to the business owner because they know the site intimately — but a first-time visitor has no such familiarity.

**Trust leaks** occur when a website fails to establish credibility. Visitors are making a judgement about whether to trust a business within seconds of arriving. Missing reviews, outdated testimonials, no visible team or location information, and an unprofessional design all erode trust before a single word is read.

**Technical leaks** occur when underlying website infrastructure is broken, outdated, or insecure. These include missing SSL certificates, broken links, outdated plugins, and indexing errors that prevent the site from appearing in search results.

**Conversion leaks** occur when a website fails to guide visitors toward taking action — making an enquiry, booking a call, or requesting a quote. Even a well-designed, fast, and trustworthy website will underperform if it does not make the next step obvious and frictionless.

## Chapter 4 — The 27-Point Website Audit Checklist

Work through each item below. Mark each as **Pass**, **Partial**, or **Fail**, and note any immediate actions in the space provided.

### HOMEPAGE (Items 1–6)

---

**Item 1 — Is your value proposition visible within 5 seconds?**

**Why it matters:** When a visitor lands on your homepage, they make a decision to stay or leave within approximately 5 seconds. If they cannot immediately understand what you do and who you serve, they leave. This is the single most common reason websites fail to convert visitors.

**How to test it:** Open your homepage on a device you have not used recently (to avoid cached familiarity). Set a 5-second timer. When it expires, close the tab and write down what you understood about the business. If you cannot clearly articulate the business's core offering and target customer from those 5 seconds, the value proposition is not clear enough.

**What good looks like:** A headline that names the problem you solve or the outcome you deliver, followed by a one-sentence subheading that clarifies who you serve and where. Example: *"We build websites that generate leads for trades businesses in Melbourne."*

---

**Item 2 — Is there a clear call to action above the fold?**

**Why it matters:** "Above the fold" refers to the portion of the page visible without scrolling. If a visitor has to scroll to find out how to contact you or take the next step, many will not bother. Your primary CTA — whether that is "Get a Free Quote," "Book a Call," or "Request a Consultation" — must be immediately visible.

**How to test it:** Open your homepage on both a desktop and a mobile device. Without scrolling, identify whether there is a visible, clickable button or link that invites the visitor to take action. Check that the button is visually distinct (contrasting colour, clear label) rather than a plain text link.

**What good looks like:** A single, prominent button with an action-oriented label visible on the screen without scrolling, on both desktop and mobile. The label should describe the outcome, not just the action — "Get My Free Quote" is stronger than "Contact Us."

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 3 — Does the headline explain who the service is for?**

**Why it matters:** Generic headlines like "Welcome to Our Website" or "Quality Service You Can Trust" tell visitors nothing. A headline that names the specific audience — "For Tradies Who Want More Leads" or "Accounting for Small Business Owners" — immediately confirms to the right visitor that they are in the right place.

**How to test it:** Read your homepage headline aloud. Ask: does this tell a first-time visitor exactly who this business serves? Could this headline apply to any business in any industry, or is it specific to your niche?

**What good looks like:** A headline that includes either the target audience, the problem being solved, or the specific outcome delivered. Ideally, it includes two of the three.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 4 — Is there a clear explanation of your process or how you work?**

**Why it matters:** Uncertainty is one of the biggest barriers to enquiry. Visitors who do not know what happens after they contact you are less likely to reach out. A simple "How It Works" section — even just three steps — removes this uncertainty and reduces the perceived risk of making contact.

**How to test it:** Scroll through your homepage. Is there a section that explains what happens after a visitor contacts you? Does it describe the process in simple, jargon-free language?

**What good looks like:** A three-step process section, such as: "1. Book a free call. 2. We assess your needs. 3. We deliver a tailored solution." Simple, visual, and reassuring.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 5 — Does the homepage address the visitor's primary concern or objection?**

**Why it matters:** Every potential customer has a primary concern before making contact. For a trades business, it might be "Will they show up on time?" For a legal firm, it might be "Will this cost more than I expect?" A homepage that directly addresses the most common objection converts significantly better than one that ignores it.

**How to test it:** Think about the most common question or concern you hear from new clients before they commit. Is that concern addressed anywhere on your homepage? If not, it is a conversion leak.

**What good looks like:** A brief section, testimonial, or FAQ item that directly addresses the top one or two objections your ideal client has before making contact.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 6 — Is the navigation simple and logical?**

**Why it matters:** A cluttered or confusing navigation menu forces visitors to work harder to find what they need. Every extra click or moment of confusion increases the likelihood they will leave. Navigation should reflect how visitors think about your business, not how you think about it internally.

**How to test it:** Count the number of items in your main navigation menu. More than seven items is generally too many. Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to find your contact page and your most popular service page — time how long it takes them.

**What good looks like:** Five to seven navigation items maximum, with clear, descriptive labels. The contact page should be reachable in one click from anywhere on the site.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

### SPEED (Items 7–10)

---

**Item 7 — Does your homepage load in under 3 seconds?**

**Why it matters:** Page speed is one of the most directly measurable revenue leaks. Studies consistently show that a one-second delay in page load time can reduce conversions by up to 7%. Google also uses page speed as a ranking factor, meaning a slow site is penalised in search results as well as in visitor behaviour.

**How to test it:** Visit [Google PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev) and enter your homepage URL. Note the load time score for both mobile and desktop. A score above 90 is considered good; below 50 requires urgent attention.

**What good looks like:** A mobile load time of under 3 seconds and a PageSpeed score of 80 or above on mobile. Desktop scores are typically higher and less critical, as the majority of traffic is now mobile.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 8 — Are images compressed and optimised?**

**Why it matters:** Unoptimised images are the single most common cause of slow websites. A single high-resolution photograph uploaded directly from a camera can be 5–10MB in size. The same image, properly compressed, can be under 100KB with no visible quality loss. Multiply this across a site with 20 or 30 images and the impact on load time is enormous.

**How to test it:** Right-click on the main image on your homepage and select "Inspect" or "Open image in new tab." Check the file size. Any image over 200KB on a live website is a candidate for compression. Tools like [GTmetrix](https://gtmetrix.com) will also flag oversized images automatically.

**What good looks like:** All images under 200KB, ideally under 100KB. Images saved in WebP format (a modern, highly compressed format) rather than JPEG or PNG where possible.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 9 — Are unused plugins removed?**

**Why it matters:** Every plugin installed on a WordPress website adds code that must be loaded on every page visit, whether the plugin is active or not. Unused and deactivated plugins still add weight to the site and, critically, still represent a security vulnerability if they are not updated. A site with 30 plugins is almost always slower and less secure than a site with 10.

**How to test it:** Log in to your WordPress dashboard and navigate to Plugins > Installed Plugins. Review the full list. Identify any plugins that are deactivated, duplicated in function, or no longer needed. Count the total number of active plugins — more than 20 active plugins on a small business site is worth reviewing.

**What good looks like:** Only active, necessary plugins installed. No deactivated plugins left in the system. Each plugin serves a distinct, documented purpose.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 10 — Is your hosting environment adequate for your traffic?**

**Why it matters:** Shared hosting — where your website shares a server with hundreds or thousands of other websites — is the most common cause of slow load times that cannot be fixed through optimisation alone. If your site is on a budget shared hosting plan and receiving meaningful traffic, the hosting itself may be the bottleneck.

**How to test it:** Contact your hosting provider and ask what type of hosting plan you are on. If the answer is "shared hosting" and your plan costs less than $15 per month, it is likely a performance constraint. GTmetrix's "Time to First Byte" (TTFB) metric is a reliable indicator of hosting performance — a TTFB above 600ms suggests a hosting upgrade is warranted.

**What good looks like:** A managed WordPress hosting plan or a VPS (Virtual Private Server) for sites with meaningful traffic. A TTFB under 200ms.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

### TRUST (Items 11–15)

---

**Item 11 — Are Google reviews visible on the website?**

**Why it matters:** Social proof is one of the most powerful conversion drivers available to a small business. A visitor who arrives from a Google search has likely already seen your star rating — but a visitor who arrives from a referral or social media has not. Displaying your Google reviews directly on the website ensures every visitor benefits from that social proof, regardless of how they found you.

**How to test it:** Browse your website as a first-time visitor. Are your Google reviews visible without navigating away from the site? Are they current (within the last 12 months)?

**What good looks like:** A reviews widget or embedded testimonial section on the homepage displaying at least five recent Google reviews, with star ratings visible. Ideally, reviews are also present on key service pages.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 12 — Are client logos or partner logos displayed?**

**Why it matters:** Logos of recognisable clients, partners, or industry associations serve as instant credibility signals. They communicate that other businesses have trusted you — and that you are established enough to have relationships worth displaying. Even for small businesses, a row of client logos on the homepage significantly increases perceived authority.

**How to test it:** Review your homepage and key service pages. Are any client, partner, or association logos displayed? If not, consider whether you have permission to display logos from clients you have worked with.

**What good looks like:** A "Trusted By" or "Our Clients" section on the homepage featuring five to ten recognisable logos. For businesses without notable clients, industry association memberships or certifications serve the same purpose.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 13 — Are testimonials recent and specific?**

**Why it matters:** Generic testimonials ("Great service, highly recommend!") add little value. Specific testimonials that describe a real problem, the solution provided, and the outcome achieved are significantly more persuasive. Equally, testimonials that are several years old raise questions about whether the business is still operating at the same standard.

**How to test it:** Read each testimonial on your website. Does each one describe a specific outcome or experience? Is each one dated or attributable to a named individual or business? Are any testimonials more than three years old?

**What good looks like:** Testimonials that include the client's name, business name or industry, and a specific description of the result achieved. All testimonials should be from the last two to three years.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 14 — Is there a visible physical address or service area?**

**Why it matters:** Many visitors — particularly those searching for local services — want to know where a business is based before making contact. A website with no location information feels anonymous and unaccountable. Even service businesses that operate across a wide area should clearly state their base location and service coverage.

**How to test it:** Check your website's footer, contact page, and homepage. Is a physical address, suburb, or service area clearly stated? Is it easy to find without searching?

**What good looks like:** A physical address or clearly stated service area in the footer of every page, and on the contact page. For local businesses, an embedded Google Map on the contact page adds further credibility.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 15 — Does the website have a valid SSL certificate?**

**Why it matters:** An SSL certificate encrypts the connection between your website and its visitors, protecting any data submitted through forms. More practically, websites without SSL display a "Not Secure" warning in the browser address bar — a trust signal that causes many visitors to leave immediately. Google also penalises non-SSL sites in search rankings.

**How to test it:** Look at your website's URL in the browser. It should begin with "https://" rather than "http://". You can also use [SSL Labs](https://www.ssllabs.com/ssltest/) to run a full certificate check. Ensure the certificate is not expired — an expired SSL certificate is as damaging as having none at all.

**What good looks like:** A valid, current SSL certificate with an "https://" URL. An SSL Labs grade of A or B.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

### UX — USER EXPERIENCE (Items 16–19)

---

**Item 16 — Is the website fully functional on mobile devices?**

**Why it matters:** In Australia, mobile devices account for more than 60% of all web traffic. A website that is difficult to use on a phone — with text too small to read, buttons too close together to tap, or images that overflow the screen — is effectively broken for the majority of its visitors.

**How to test it:** Open your website on your smartphone and navigate through the homepage, a service page, and the contact page. Can you read all text without zooming? Can you tap all buttons and links without difficulty? Does the layout adjust cleanly to the smaller screen? You can also use Google's Mobile-Friendly Test tool for a technical assessment.

**What good looks like:** A fully responsive design that adapts cleanly to all screen sizes. Text readable without zooming. Buttons and links large enough to tap comfortably. No horizontal scrolling required.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 17 — Can a visitor contact you in under 2 clicks from any page?**

**Why it matters:** Friction kills conversions. Every additional step between a visitor's decision to make contact and the moment they can actually do so is an opportunity for them to change their mind. Your contact page, phone number, or enquiry form should be reachable from anywhere on the site in no more than two clicks.

**How to test it:** Starting from your homepage, count the clicks required to reach your contact form. Then repeat from a service page and a blog post. Is the contact option always within two clicks? Is your phone number visible in the header or footer of every page?

**What good looks like:** A phone number or contact link visible in the header or footer of every page. The contact page reachable in one click from anywhere on the site. A "sticky" header that remains visible as the visitor scrolls is ideal.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 18 — Is there a CTA on every key page?**

**Why it matters:** Many websites have a strong CTA on the homepage but forget to include one on service pages, blog posts, and the about page. A visitor who reads your about page and is impressed by your story should have an immediate, obvious way to take the next step — not have to navigate back to the homepage to find a contact button.

**How to test it:** Visit each of your main pages — homepage, each service page, about page, and contact page. Does each page include at least one clear, visible CTA? Is the CTA relevant to the content on that page?

**What good looks like:** Every page ends with a CTA section. Service pages include a CTA specific to that service (e.g., "Get a Quote for Electrical Work"). Blog posts include a CTA relevant to the topic discussed.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 19 — Is the font size and colour contrast readable?**

**Why it matters:** Poor readability is a silent conversion killer. Small fonts, low-contrast text (such as light grey on white), and decorative fonts that are difficult to read at small sizes all increase cognitive load and reduce the time visitors spend engaging with your content.

**How to test it:** View your website on a mobile device in normal lighting conditions. Can you read body text comfortably without squinting? Check the contrast ratio of your body text using the [WebAIM Contrast Checker](https://webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker/). A ratio of at least 4.5:1 is the minimum standard for accessibility.

**What good looks like:** Body text of at least 16px on mobile. A contrast ratio of 4.5:1 or higher for all body text. Headings clearly distinguished from body text in size and weight.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

### CONVERSION (Items 20–23)

---

**Item 20 — Is the contact form under 5 fields?**

**Why it matters:** Every additional field in a contact form reduces the likelihood of completion. Research consistently shows that forms with three to five fields convert significantly better than those with seven or more. Many business owners add fields because they want more information upfront — but the cost is fewer enquiries overall.

**How to test it:** Count the number of fields in your contact or enquiry form. Are any fields optional that could be removed entirely? Are there fields asking for information you do not actually use?

**What good looks like:** A contact form with three to five fields: name, email, phone (optional), and a brief message or service selection. Budget or project details can be gathered in the follow-up conversation.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 21 — Is there a confirmation message after form submission?**

**Why it matters:** A visitor who submits a contact form and sees nothing happen — no confirmation message, no redirect to a thank-you page — does not know whether their enquiry was received. Many will submit the form again, creating duplicate enquiries, or assume it failed and call instead. A missing confirmation message also prevents you from tracking conversions in Google Analytics.

**How to test it:** Submit a test enquiry through your contact form. Does a confirmation message appear immediately? Does it tell the visitor when they can expect a response? Is there a thank-you page URL that can be tracked as a conversion event?

**What good looks like:** An immediate on-page confirmation message or redirect to a dedicated thank-you page. The confirmation should set expectations: "Thank you — we will be in touch within one business day."

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 22 — Is there a compelling reason to contact you now rather than later?**

**Why it matters:** Without urgency or a clear incentive, visitors who are interested but not yet ready will bookmark your site and never return. A reason to act now — whether that is a limited availability notice, a free consultation offer, or a specific outcome they will receive by making contact — increases the conversion rate of visitors who are on the fence.

**How to test it:** Read your CTA buttons and surrounding copy. Is there any language that creates a reason to act today rather than next week? Is there a specific offer, benefit, or outcome associated with making contact?

**What good looks like:** CTA copy that includes a benefit or incentive: "Book a Free 30-Minute Website Review" is stronger than "Contact Us." A note about response time ("We respond within 24 hours") reduces the perceived risk of reaching out.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 23 — Is Google Analytics (or equivalent) installed and tracking correctly?**

**Why it matters:** You cannot improve what you do not measure. Without analytics, you have no way of knowing how many people visit your site, which pages they visit, how long they stay, or where they come from. This makes every decision about your website a guess.

**How to test it:** Visit [Google Analytics](https://analytics.google.com) and check whether your website is sending data. If you are unsure whether Analytics is installed, use the [Tag Assistant](https://tagassistant.google.com/) Chrome extension to check. Verify that goal or conversion tracking is set up for form submissions.

**What good looks like:** Google Analytics 4 installed and receiving data. At least one conversion event configured (typically form submission or phone click). Monthly review of key metrics including sessions, bounce rate, and top landing pages.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

### TECHNICAL (Items 24–27)

---

**Item 24 — Are there any broken links on the website?**

**Why it matters:** Broken links — pages that return a 404 error — damage user experience, erode trust, and are penalised by Google in search rankings. They are particularly common on sites that have been updated over time without a systematic review of internal links.

**How to test it:** Use the free version of [Screaming Frog SEO Spider](https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/seo-spider/) to crawl your website and identify any 404 errors. Alternatively, Google Search Console's "Coverage" report will flag pages that are returning errors.

**What good looks like:** Zero 404 errors on internal links. Any removed or renamed pages should have a 301 redirect in place pointing to the most relevant current page.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 25 — Is the website being monitored for downtime?**

**Why it matters:** Websites go offline. Hosting servers experience outages, plugins conflict and crash the site, and domain renewals are missed. Without uptime monitoring, you may not know your website has been down for hours or days — and neither will the visitors who tried to reach you during that time.

**How to test it:** Check whether you have an uptime monitoring service configured. If you receive no alerts when your website goes offline, you have no monitoring in place.

**What good looks like:** A free uptime monitoring service such as [UptimeRobot](https://uptimerobot.com) configured to check your site every five minutes and send an email or SMS alert if it goes offline.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 26 — Are all plugins and the CMS platform up to date?**

**Why it matters:** Outdated plugins and CMS platforms (such as WordPress) are the most common entry point for website hacks. Security vulnerabilities are discovered regularly, and updates are released to patch them. A site running outdated software is not a question of if it will be compromised — it is a question of when.

**How to test it:** Log in to your WordPress dashboard. Navigate to Dashboard > Updates. Are there any pending plugin, theme, or core updates? How many, and how long have they been pending?

**What good looks like:** All plugins, themes, and the CMS core updated within the last 30 days. Automatic updates enabled for minor security releases. A staging environment used to test major updates before applying them to the live site.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

---

**Item 27 — Is there a recent, tested backup of the website?**

**Why it matters:** Without a current backup, a hacked, crashed, or accidentally deleted website can mean days or weeks of lost work — or a complete rebuild from scratch. Backups are the single most underrated form of website insurance.

**How to test it:** Ask your hosting provider or developer when the last backup was taken and where it is stored. Critically, ask whether the backup has been tested — a backup that cannot be successfully restored is worthless.

**What good looks like:** Daily automated backups stored in a location separate from the hosting server (such as cloud storage). A tested restore process confirmed within the last six months.

☐ Pass &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Partial &nbsp;&nbsp; ☐ Fail

## Chapter 6 — The Revenue Leak Scorecard

Tally your results from the 27-point checklist using the scoring system below.

**Scoring:**
- Pass = 2 points
- Partial = 1 point
- Fail = 0 points

**Maximum score: 54 points**

| Score Range | Rating | What It Means |

## Chapter 8 — Red Flags That Require Professional Help

Some website issues cannot — and should not — be addressed without professional involvement. Attempting to fix the following problems without the right expertise can make the situation significantly worse, including breaking the site entirely or creating new security vulnerabilities.

**Your theme is no longer supported by its developer.** When a WordPress theme is abandoned by its creator, it stops receiving security patches and compatibility updates. Over time, it will conflict with updated plugins and the WordPress core, causing errors and vulnerabilities. A theme swap is not a simple task — it typically requires a full rebuild to avoid breaking the site's layout and functionality. This is not a DIY fix.

**You rely on outdated or abandoned plugins.** Plugins that no longer receive updates are among the most common entry points for malicious attacks on WordPress websites. Removing them without suitable replacements can break core site functionality — contact forms, booking systems, and payment gateways are often plugin-dependent. A developer needs to audit the dependency chain before making changes.

**You have page builder conflicts.** This is particularly common on sites that have been worked on by multiple developers over time, where Elementor, Divi, WPBakery, and the native WordPress block editor have all been used at different points. The result is bloated, conflicting code that cannot be resolved by simply deleting plugins — doing so typically breaks the page layout entirely.

**Your site speed cannot be improved further within the current environment.** Some websites are structurally too heavy to optimise without a rebuild. Caching plugins, image compression, and CDN configuration will only go so far. If your PageSpeed score remains below 50 after standard optimisation, the issue is likely architectural — the theme, the page builder, or the hosting environment itself. A developer needs to assess whether optimisation is viable or whether a rebuild is more cost-effective.

**Core Web Vitals are failing despite optimisation attempts.** Google's Core Web Vitals — Largest Contentful Paint, Cumulative Layout Shift, and Interaction to Next Paint — are technical performance metrics that directly affect search rankings. Persistent failures in these metrics, despite standard optimisation, indicate deep structural issues that require a developer to resolve.

**The site was built by a developer who is no longer contactable.** If you do not have access to your own hosting account, domain registrar, and CMS login — or if no documentation was handed over — you are in a vulnerable position. A developer needs to audit what access you have, what you are missing, and how to regain control of your own digital assets.

**You have had more than two developers work on the site with no documentation.** Each developer typically adds their own plugins, customisations, and code snippets. Without documentation, the site becomes a black box that no one fully understands. Making changes in this environment carries significant risk of unintended consequences.

> If you identified three or more of these red flags in your own website, a professional website audit or rebuild conversation is worth having. The cost of professional help is almost always less than the cost of continued underperformance — or the cost of recovering from a security breach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**I am not technical — can I still use this workbook?**
Yes. This workbook is designed specifically for non-technical business owners. Every diagnostic question is written in plain English, and where a tool is needed to test something, it is named and the process is described. You do not need to understand how websites are built to use this workbook effectively.

**How long will it take to complete?**
Allow 90 to 120 minutes for the full workbook, including the competitor benchmarking worksheet. You can complete it in sections if needed, but working through it in one sitting produces the clearest picture.

**What if I score very low — does that mean my website is beyond saving?**
Not necessarily. A low score means your website has significant room for improvement — which is actually a positive finding. It means there are concrete, actionable changes that can meaningfully improve your results. The workbook helps you understand where to focus, not whether to give up.

**Do I need to hire someone to fix everything I identify?**
No. Many items in this workbook can be addressed independently, particularly in the areas of messaging, content, trust signals, and basic technical maintenance. The workbook flags which issues typically require professional involvement — and the "When to Walk Away" assessment helps you determine whether a rebuild is warranted.

**How often should I run this diagnostic?**
Annually, or after any significant change to your business, website, or market. A website that was performing well 18 months ago may have fallen behind as competitors have improved their own sites, as Google's ranking factors have evolved, or as your business has grown and changed.

Bring your completed Health Score Summary Page and Post-Workbook Action Plan to a conversation with a web professional. These documents give a developer immediate, specific insight into your situation — which makes the conversation more productive and the recommendations more targeted.

### 10 Questions to Ask Any Web Developer Before You Hire Them

These questions are designed to reveal a developer's process, professionalism, and suitability for your project. A developer who cannot answer them clearly and confidently is not ready to build your website.

**1. Can I see examples of websites you have built for businesses similar to mine?**
Industry-relevant examples demonstrate that the developer understands your audience and the type of content and functionality your site will require.

**2. Who will own the website and domain when the project is complete?**
You should own everything — the domain, the hosting account, and the website files. Any developer who retains ownership of your website as a condition of the engagement is a significant red flag.

**3. What platform will the site be built on, and why?**
The developer should be able to explain their platform recommendation in plain English and justify it based on your specific needs — not simply because it is the only platform they work with.

**4. What does the handover process look like?**
A professional developer will provide you with all login credentials, a brief guide to managing the site yourself, and documentation of any custom code or configurations. Ask for this in writing before signing any agreement.

**5. Will I be able to edit the site myself after launch?**
For most small business websites, the answer should be yes. If a developer builds a site that requires their involvement for every minor content update, you will be perpetually dependent on them — and paying for it.

**6. What ongoing support do you offer after launch?**
Websites require ongoing maintenance. Understand what is included in the project price and what will be charged additionally. Is there a maintenance plan available? What is the response time for urgent issues?

**7. Do you use a staging environment for development and testing?**
A staging environment is a private copy of the website where changes are built and tested before being applied to the live site. Developers who work directly on live sites are taking unnecessary risks with your business.

**8. How do you handle SEO as part of the build?**
At minimum, a new website should be built with clean URL structures, proper heading hierarchy, meta title and description fields for every page, and submission to Google Search Console. Ask specifically what SEO work is included in the scope.

**9. What is your process for managing project scope and timeline?**
Scope creep — where the project expands beyond the original brief without a corresponding adjustment to price or timeline — is one of the most common sources of conflict in web development projects. Ask how the developer manages change requests and timeline slippage.

**10. Can you provide references from two recent clients?**
A confident, established developer will have no hesitation providing references. Speaking directly with a previous client gives you insight into the developer's communication style, reliability, and the quality of their post-launch support.

### The 7 Most Common Website Traps

These are the experiences that business owners describe most frequently when recounting a bad website project. Knowing them in advance is the best protection against them.

**Trap 1 — Being locked into a proprietary platform.**
Some developers build websites on platforms they control — either their own proprietary CMS or a hosted website builder that cannot be migrated. If you ever want to change developers or move your site, you may find that your website cannot be transferred. Always confirm that your website can be moved to a different host or developer if needed.

**Trap 2 — Paying for a site that was never properly handed over.**
Many business owners discover — often when something goes wrong — that they do not have access to their own hosting account, domain registrar, or CMS. Insist on receiving all login credentials and access details at the time of project completion, not when you ask for them later.

**Trap 3 — Being sold a template disguised as a custom design.**
A template-based website is not inherently a bad product — but it should be priced accordingly and disclosed upfront. If you are paying $8,000 for a website and receiving a lightly modified $60 theme, you are not getting what you paid for. Ask to see the theme or framework the design is based on.

**Trap 4 — No staging environment.**
Developers who make changes directly to your live website are one mistake away from taking your site offline. A staging environment is a standard professional practice, not an optional extra. If a developer does not use one, find a different developer.

**Trap 5 — Ongoing fees for work that was never performed.**
Some developers charge monthly "maintenance" or "hosting" fees that include services they never actually deliver — backups that are never taken, updates that are never applied, and security monitoring that never happens. Ask for a monthly report confirming what maintenance work was performed.

**Trap 6 — Contracts with no clear deliverables or timelines.**
A contract that describes the project in vague terms — "a professional website for your business" — gives you no recourse if the result does not meet your expectations. Insist on a detailed scope of work that lists every page, feature, and deliverable, along with a project timeline with milestones.

**Trap 7 — Choosing a developer based on price alone.**
The cheapest quote is rarely the best value. A website built poorly will cost you more to fix than it would have cost to build correctly in the first place — and in the meantime, it will be costing you leads. Evaluate developers on their portfolio, their process, their communication, and their references — not their price.

Platforms like Squarespace, Wix, and Shopify have made it genuinely possible for non-technical business owners to build a functional website without a developer. | Factor | DIY Platform | Professional Developer |

## Bonus 2 — The Website Maintenance Checklist

A website is not a set-and-forget asset. Like any business tool, it requires regular maintenance to remain secure, performant, and effective. Use the checklist below to establish a maintenance routine.

### Monthly Maintenance Tasks

- Check for and apply all pending plugin, theme, and CMS updates (use a staging environment for major updates)
- Test the contact form by submitting a test enquiry and confirming receipt
- Check Google Search Console for any new errors, manual actions, or coverage issues
- Review Google Analytics for any unusual drops in traffic or engagement
- Confirm that automated backups are running and that recent backup files exist
- Check UptimeRobot or your monitoring service for any recorded downtime events
- Review and respond to any new Google reviews

### Quarterly Maintenance Tasks

- Run a full PageSpeed Insights test on the homepage and top five pages; note any score changes
- Run a Screaming Frog crawl to identify any new broken links or redirect errors
- Review the SSL certificate expiry date and confirm auto-renewal is in place
- Check all external links on the website to confirm they are still active
- Review and update testimonials — replace any that are more than two years old
- Review the homepage and key service pages for any outdated information (pricing, team members, services offered)
- Run a malware scan using Sucuri SiteCheck

### Annual Maintenance Tasks

- Conduct a full website audit using this guide
- Review the website's design against current competitor and industry standards
- Assess whether the website's structure and content still reflect the current business strategy
- Review the hosting plan and assess whether an upgrade is warranted based on traffic growth
- Confirm that all login credentials are documented and stored securely
- Review and update the privacy policy and terms of use
- Assess whether any new functionality — booking systems, live chat, e-commerce — would meaningfully improve the website's performance

## Glossary of Terms

**Above the fold:** The portion of a web page visible without scrolling. Originates from newspaper publishing, where the most important stories appeared above the physical fold of the paper.

**Bounce rate:** The percentage of visitors who leave a website after viewing only one page, without taking any action. A high bounce rate often indicates a mismatch between what the visitor expected and what the page delivered.

**Call to action (CTA):** A prompt that encourages a visitor to take a specific action — such as "Get a Free Quote," "Book a Call," or "Download the Guide."

**CDN (Content Delivery Network):** A network of servers distributed across multiple geographic locations that delivers website files to visitors from the server closest to them, improving load times.

**Conversion rate:** The percentage of website visitors who complete a desired action, such as submitting an enquiry form or clicking a phone number.

**Core Web Vitals:** A set of metrics defined by Google to measure the real-world user experience of a web page, including load performance, visual stability, and interactivity.

**CMS (Content Management System):** Software that allows website content to be created and managed without technical knowledge. WordPress is the most widely used CMS globally.

**Meta description:** A short summary of a web page's content that appears beneath the page title in search engine results. Does not directly affect rankings but influences click-through rates.

**Plugin:** A software add-on that extends the functionality of a CMS such as WordPress. Plugins can add features ranging from contact forms to e-commerce to security monitoring.

**Responsive design:** A website design approach that ensures the layout and content adapt correctly to different screen sizes, including desktop, tablet, and mobile.

**SEO (Search Engine Optimisation):** The practice of improving a website's visibility in organic (non-paid) search engine results.

**SSL certificate:** A digital certificate that encrypts the connection between a website and its visitors. Websites with SSL display "https://" in the browser address bar.

**Staging environment:** A private copy of a website used for development and testing before changes are applied to the live site.

**Title tag:** The text that appears in the browser tab and in search engine results as the clickable headline for a page. One of the most important on-page SEO elements.

**UX (User Experience):** The overall experience a person has when interacting with a website, including ease of navigation, clarity of information, and efficiency of completing tasks.

**Value proposition:** A clear statement of the specific benefit a business delivers to its customers, and why they should choose it over alternatives.

**WebP:** A modern image format developed by Google that provides superior compression compared to JPEG and PNG, resulting in smaller file sizes with comparable visual quality.

**404 error:** An HTTP status code indicating that the requested page could not be found. Typically caused by deleted pages, changed URLs, or broken internal links.

**301 redirect:** A permanent redirect from one URL to another. Used when a page is moved or deleted to ensure visitors and search engines are directed to the correct destination.
