Chapter 1

Warning Signs Your Site Has Been Hacked

Hackers are often invisible for weeks or months. They want to use your site quietly — for spam, malicious content, or cryptocurrency mining. Even one of the following signs should be treated as a serious red flag.

  • Visitors are being redirected to other websites — often only triggered from Google searches, so you may see nothing wrong on your own visits.
  • Google is showing a security warning — "This site may harm your computer" or "This site may be hacked" appearing in search results.
  • Your site displays content you did not create — spam links, foreign-language text, or new pages promoting pharmaceuticals or adult content.
  • Your site has disappeared from Google results — de-indexing caused by detected malware or policy violations.
  • Your hosting company has suspended your account — reputable hosts actively scan for malware and suspend infected sites.
  • Unusual slowness or frequent crashes — server resources being consumed by spam-sending or cryptocurrency mining.
  • Customers receiving suspicious emails from your domain — hackers may be spoofing or using your email server.
  • Files modified without your knowledge — unfamiliar file names or recently modified dates on core files are strong indicators.
Chapter 1

The 5 Most Common Types of Website Hack

Understanding the specific type of hack your site has experienced is important — different hacks require different responses, and some are significantly more damaging than others.

Pharma Hack (SEO Spam)
Thousands of hidden pages injected, invisible to you but visible to Google — destroying your search rankings.
Redirect Hack
Visitors from Google are silently sent to scam or adult sites. You see nothing wrong on direct visits.
Backdoor
A hidden re-entry point left by hackers. Even after cleanup, they return within 24–48 hours if the backdoor is missed.
Cryptojacking
Code injected to mine cryptocurrency using your visitors' computers. No visible damage — extremely hard to detect.
The 5th Type: Defacement Attack

The most visible hack — attackers replace your website content with their own message. Immediately obvious and deeply damaging to brand credibility, but at least it's discovered quickly.

Chapter 2

The 7 Immediate Steps — Acting Quickly Is Everything

The moment you confirm or strongly suspect a hack, the clock starts. The following seven steps should be completed as quickly as possible — ideally within the first 24 hours.

Step 1: Put Your Website Into Maintenance Mode
Your first priority is to stop the bleeding. Log into your CMS and activate maintenance mode to replace your site with a simple "We're currently undergoing maintenance" message. The reputational cost of a brief maintenance period is far smaller than customers discovering your site tried to install malware on their computer.
Step 2: Change Every Password Associated With Your Website
Change your CMS admin password, hosting control panel, FTP/SFTP, database password, and any other admin user accounts. Use a minimum of 16 characters with upper/lowercase, numbers, and symbols. Use a password manager (1Password, Bitwarden) to generate and store these securely.
Step 3: Contact Your Web Hosting Provider
Inform them of the compromise. Ask them to: review server logs to identify the attack source; scan for malware; restore from a clean backup if available; and isolate your account to prevent spread. Ask explicitly for the server access logs for the past 30 days.
Chapter 2

Steps 4 to 7

Step 4: Scan Your Website for Malware
Run a dedicated security scan using Wordfence or MalCare (WordPress) or Sucuri (all platforms). The scan will produce a report listing infected files and suspicious code. Save this report — you may need it if required to report to the OAIC.
Step 5: Remove the Malware and Restore Clean Files
Replace infected core files with clean versions. A critical warning: DIY malware removal misses backdoors — hidden files designed to evade basic scanning. Industry data shows a significant proportion of DIY-cleaned sites are re-hacked within 30 days. Professional removal is strongly recommended.
Step 6: Identify and Close the Vulnerability
Removing malware is not enough. Unless you close the entry point, your site will be compromised again — often within hours. Review server logs and the malware report to identify the specific file or vulnerability exploited.
Step 7: Notify Affected Parties
Notify customers if personal information may have been accessed (see Chapter 4 for legal obligations). Submit a review request to Google via Search Console to remove security warnings. Notify your email list if your domain was used to send spam.

Chapter 3

Why Hackers Targeted Your Site — It Was Not Personal

One of the first questions business owners ask after a hack is: "Why me?" The answer, in most cases, is not that your business was specifically targeted. Hackers — particularly the automated bots that conduct the majority of attacks — do not discriminate by industry, size, or location. They scan millions of websites continuously, looking for known vulnerabilities, and attack whatever they find.

The Uncomfortable Truth

You were not targeted because of who you are. You were targeted because of what your website was: vulnerable.

Understanding the specific reasons your site was vulnerable is the most important step in preventing future attacks. The following are the seven most common root causes — examine each honestly against your own situation.

Chapter 3

Root Causes 1–3: Software, Plugins & Hosting

Root Cause 1: Outdated CMS (Most Common)

WordPress releases regular updates that patch known security vulnerabilities. When those updates are not applied, those vulnerabilities remain open indefinitely. Hackers maintain databases of known vulnerabilities in every version and automated bots begin scanning for unpatched sites within hours of a vulnerability being published.

Root Cause 2: Outdated or Abandoned Plugins

Plugin vulnerabilities account for the majority of WordPress hacking incidents. Particularly dangerous are abandoned plugins — those whose developers have stopped releasing updates. Check the WordPress.org repository: any plugin not updated in 12+ months should be treated with caution; any not updated in 2+ years should be removed.

Root Cause 3: Cheap or Shared Hosting

On cheap shared hosting, if any one of the hundreds or thousands of other websites on your server is compromised, malware can spread to your site. Ask your host: "Do you use account isolation on your shared hosting servers?" Many budget providers do not implement proper isolation. This alone can re-infect a recently cleaned site.

Chapter 3

Root Causes 4–7: Access, Code, SSL & Monitoring

Root Cause 4: Weak Passwords & Poor Access Control

Using "admin" as a WordPress username, weak passwords, giving admin access to former staff or contractors, and never enabling two-factor authentication are all common failures. Brute-force attacks try thousands of combinations per second.

Root Cause 5: Poorly Coded Original Build

Websites built without security best practices contain vulnerabilities like SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS), and insecure file upload handlers. These cannot be fixed by updating plugins — the underlying code must be rewritten.

Root Cause 6: No SSL or Misconfigured Security Headers

Without SSL, data is transmitted in plain text. Without HTTP security headers (Content Security Policy, HSTS, X-Frame-Options), your site is vulnerable to browser-based attacks. Run your site through securityheaders.com — most small business sites receive an "F" grade.

Root Cause 7: No Active Monitoring

A website with no daily malware scanning, file integrity monitoring, or login attempt monitoring will not detect a compromise until the damage is already done — often weeks later. Monitoring tools are inexpensive and entirely preventable.

Chapter 4

Your Legal Obligations After a Website Hack

Australia has clear, enforceable legal obligations around data breach notification. The penalties for non-compliance have increased dramatically in recent years. This is not optional.

The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) Scheme

Established under the Privacy Act 1988, the NDB scheme requires organisations to notify the OAIC and affected individuals when an "eligible data breach" occurs — one that is likely to result in serious harm including financial, reputational, physical, or psychological harm.

QuestionAnswer
Who is covered?Businesses with annual turnover over $3M, all health service providers, and certain other categories. Businesses below this threshold may still have contractual obligations.
What counts as a breach?If your website stores personal information (names, emails, payment details, health info) and that information may have been accessed by an unauthorised party.
Notification deadlineNotify the OAIC as soon as practicable — ideally within 30 days of becoming aware of the breach.
Penalties (December 2022 update)Up to $50 million, or 3× the value of the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover. Smaller businesses: up to $2.5M per breach.
Chapter 4

Breach Response Steps & The Reputational Cost

The Required Process Under NDB

  • Assess the breach — What information was accessed? How many individuals? Is serious harm likely?
  • Contain the breach — Take the site offline, change passwords, remove malicious code.
  • Notify the OAIC — Use the online form at oaic.gov.au. Describe the breach, information involved, and response steps.
  • Notify affected individuals — Direct contact (email/letter) or a prominent notice on your website if direct contact is not practicable.
  • Review and remediate — Document everything. This documentation will be required if the OAIC investigates.

Chapter 5

Temporary Fixes vs. The Permanent Solution

When a business owner discovers their site has been hacked, the natural instinct is to fix it as quickly and cheaply as possible. This is understandable — but it is, in most cases, a decision that will lead to being hacked again.

The Analogy

Imagine your home was broken into through a window with a faulty lock. You fix that lock — but the burglar comes back through a different window with the same faulty lock, or through the back door that was never properly secured. Cleaning malware is fixing the lock. It does not address the structural vulnerabilities.

What a Patching Exercise Misses

  • It cannot fix vulnerabilities in the original code — SQL injection, XSS, and insecure upload handlers require a code rewrite, not a plugin update.
  • It cannot guarantee all malware is found — Sophisticated hackers plant multiple backdoors. A significant proportion of cleaned sites are re-hacked within 30 days.
  • It cannot address cheap shared hosting — If the hosting environment is compromised, cleaning the site does not prevent re-infection.
  • It cannot compensate for years of deferred maintenance — A site neglected for 3–5 years has accumulated technical debt that a patch exercise cannot clear.
  • It cannot future-proof the site — Old technology stacks continue accumulating new vulnerabilities indefinitely.

Chapter 6

What a Secure, Modern Website Actually Looks Like

A well-built website has a set of technical and security characteristics that are invisible to the casual observer but absolutely critical to its long-term performance and safety.

Minimal Plugin Stack
Only essential, actively maintained plugins. Every plugin is an additional attack surface.
Quality Hosting
Managed WordPress hosting with server-level firewalls, account isolation, daily backups, and malware scanning.
HTTPS + Security Headers
Every page served over HTTPS with a valid auto-renewing SSL. Full HTTP security header suite scoring A or A+ on securityheaders.com.
Active Monitoring
Daily malware scanning, file integrity monitoring, login attempt blocking, uptime monitoring, and Google Search Console alerts.
Regular Managed Updates

Core CMS, plugin, and theme updates applied monthly (or faster for critical patches), tested in a staging environment before going live.

Secure Access Management

Minimal admin accounts, strong unique passwords, 2FA on all admin logins, and former staff/contractors removed promptly.

Chapter 7

How to Choose the Right Web Developer

One of the most common mistakes after a hack is engaging the first developer who offers to fix it quickly and cheaply. A professional agency will answer these questions confidently and specifically. Vague or evasive answers are red flags.

Questions to Ask About Security Practices

  • "What security measures do you build into every website by default?" — Look for specific answers about security headers, hardened configurations, and minimal plugin use. Not just "we follow best practices."
  • "What hosting environment do you recommend, and why?" — They should have a considered recommendation with specific security advantages named.
  • "How do you handle updates and maintenance after launch?" — "We build the site and then it's up to you" is a significant red flag.
  • "Do you implement two-factor authentication?" — This should be standard practice, not an optional extra.
  • "How do you handle backups?" — Automated daily backups, off-site storage, and a tested restore process are the minimum standard.

Questions About Track Record

  • "Who owns the website and all its files when the project is complete?" — The answer must be: you do, unambiguously.
  • "Have any of your client websites been hacked? How did you respond?" — Every experienced developer has had a client site compromised. What matters is their response process.

Chapter 9

The Long-Term Security Plan: Security Is a Practice, Not a Project

The most important mindset shift: stop thinking of website security as a one-time project and start thinking of it as an ongoing practice. New vulnerabilities are discovered in WordPress, plugins, and themes every week. New attack techniques emerge. The only way to maintain a genuinely secure website is to treat security as a continuous, managed process.

The Four Pillars of Long-Term Website Security

Pillar 1: Prevention
A secure foundation — quality hosting, minimal plugins, clean code, and proper configuration — dramatically reduces the attack surface. Prevention is the most cost-effective form of security.
Pillar 2: Detection
Active monitoring — daily malware scanning, file integrity monitoring, login attempt monitoring — ensures that if something goes wrong, it is detected quickly. The difference between a hack discovered in hours versus months is enormous.
Pillar 3: Response
A documented incident response plan — knowing exactly what to do, in what order, and who to call — dramatically reduces the time and cost of recovery. The steps in Chapter 2 form the basis of your response plan.
Pillar 4: Recovery
Reliable, tested backups are the ultimate safety net. No matter what happens — a hack, a failed update, an accidental deletion — a clean, recent backup means you can restore quickly. Backups never tested are not reliable backups.
