Chapter 2

The 6 Ways Websites Lose Revenue

Most website problems fall into one of six categories. Understanding the category helps you prioritise fixes and decide what to address yourself versus what needs a professional.

Messaging Leaks
Visitors can't quickly understand what you do or why to choose you. The fix is almost always in the words, not the design.
Speed Leaks
Pages taking more than 3 seconds to load. Particularly damaging on mobile, where the majority of traffic now originates.
UX Leaks
Visitors can't easily navigate or find what they need. Often invisible to the owner who knows the site intimately.
Trust Leaks
Missing reviews, outdated testimonials, no visible team — all erode trust before a single word is read.
Technical Leaks
Broken SSL, broken links, outdated plugins, and indexing errors that prevent the site appearing in search results.
Conversion Leaks
The site fails to guide visitors toward action. Even a fast, well-designed site underperforms if the next step isn't obvious.

Chapter 4 — Homepage

27-Point Checklist: Homepage (Items 1–3)

Mark each item Pass (2 pts), Partial (1 pt), or Fail (0 pts). Your section score updates live below.

1
Is your value proposition visible within 5 seconds?
Set a 5-second timer and open your homepage on an unfamiliar device. A strong headline names the problem you solve and who you serve — e.g. "We build websites that generate leads for trades businesses in Melbourne."
2
Is there a clear call to action above the fold?
Check both desktop and mobile without scrolling. Your primary CTA must be a visible, contrasting button. "Get My Free Quote" is stronger than "Contact Us" — the label should describe the outcome, not just the action.
3
Does the headline explain who the service is for?
Read your homepage headline aloud. Could it apply to any business in any industry? A headline naming a specific audience — "For Tradies Who Want More Leads" — immediately confirms to the right visitor they're in the right place.

Is there a clear explanation of your process or how you work?

Chapter 4 — Speed

27-Point Checklist: Speed (Items 7–10)

7
Does your homepage load in under 3 seconds?
Visit pagespeed.web.dev and enter your homepage URL. A score above 90 is good; below 50 requires urgent attention. A one-second delay in load time can reduce conversions by up to 7% — and Google penalises slow sites in search rankings.
8
Are images compressed and optimised?
Unoptimised images are the single most common cause of slow websites. Any image over 200KB on a live site is a candidate for compression using TinyPNG. Images should be saved in WebP format where possible — same visual quality, far smaller file size.
9
Are unused plugins removed?
Every installed plugin adds code loaded on every page visit — active or not. Check your WordPress dashboard: Plugins → Installed Plugins. More than 20 active plugins on a small business site is worth reviewing. Deactivated plugins should be deleted entirely.
10
Is your hosting environment adequate for your traffic?
Shared hosting under $15/month is likely a performance constraint. Check GTmetrix's "Time to First Byte" (TTFB) metric — above 600ms suggests a hosting upgrade is warranted. A managed WordPress host or VPS with TTFB under 200ms is the target.

Chapter 4 — Trust

27-Point Checklist: Trust (Items 11–15)

11
Are Google reviews visible on the website?
Displaying your Google reviews directly on the website ensures every visitor benefits from social proof, regardless of how they found you — including visitors from referrals or social media who haven't seen your star rating in search.
12
Are client logos or partner logos displayed?
Logos of recognisable clients, partners, or industry associations serve as instant credibility signals. A "Trusted By" section with 5–10 logos significantly increases perceived authority.
13
Are testimonials recent and specific?
Generic testimonials ("Great service!") add little value. Specific testimonials describing a real problem, the solution, and the outcome achieved are significantly more persuasive. All testimonials should be from the last 2–3 years and attributed to named individuals.
14
Is there a visible physical address or service area?
A website with no location information feels anonymous. Even service businesses operating across a wide area should state their base location and service coverage — in the footer of every page and on the contact page.
15
Does the website have a valid SSL certificate?
The URL should begin with "https://". A "Not Secure" warning causes many visitors to leave immediately — and Google penalises non-SSL sites in search rankings. Check ssllabs.com for full certificate validation.

Chapter 4 — User Experience

27-Point Checklist: UX (Items 16–19)

16
Is the website fully functional on mobile devices?
In Australia, mobile devices account for more than 60% of all web traffic. Open your website on your smartphone — can you read all text without zooming? Can you tap all buttons without difficulty? No horizontal scrolling required?
17
Can a visitor contact you in under 2 clicks from any page?
Starting from your homepage, count the clicks to reach your contact form. Repeat from a service page and a blog post. Is the contact option always within two clicks? Is your phone number visible in the header or footer of every page?
18
Is there a CTA on every key page?
Visit your homepage, each service page, about page, and contact page. Does each include at least one clear, visible CTA? A visitor who reads your About page and is impressed should have an immediate, obvious way to take the next step.
19
Is the font size and colour contrast readable?
Body text should be at least 16px on mobile. Check contrast ratio using WebAIM Contrast Checker — a minimum of 4.5:1 is required for accessibility. Low contrast and small fonts are silent conversion killers.

Chapter 4 — Conversion

27-Point Checklist: Conversion (Items 20–23)

20
Is the contact form under 5 fields?
Every additional field reduces the likelihood of completion. A form with name, email, phone (optional), and a brief message is the ideal maximum. Budget and project details can be gathered in the follow-up conversation.
21
Is there a confirmation message after form submission?
Submit a test enquiry through your contact form right now. Does a confirmation message appear immediately? Does it set expectations? "Thank you — we will be in touch within one business day." A missing confirmation also prevents conversion tracking in Google Analytics.
22
Is there a compelling reason to contact you now rather than later?
Without urgency, interested visitors bookmark and never return. "Book a Free 30-Minute Website Review" is stronger than "Contact Us." A response time note ("We respond within 24 hours") reduces the perceived risk of reaching out.
23
Is Google Analytics (or equivalent) installed and tracking correctly?
You cannot improve what you do not measure. Visit analytics.google.com and check whether your site is sending data. Verify that at least one conversion event is configured (typically form submission or phone click).

Chapter 4 — Technical

27-Point Checklist: Technical (Items 24–27)

24
Are there any broken links on the website?
Use the free version of Screaming Frog SEO Spider to crawl your website and identify 404 errors. Alternatively, Google Search Console's "Coverage" report flags error pages. Any removed or renamed page should have a 301 redirect in place.
25
Is the website being monitored for downtime?
Set up UptimeRobot (free) to check your site every five minutes and send an email or SMS alert if it goes offline. Without monitoring, you may not know your website has been down for hours — and neither will visitors who tried to reach you.
26
Are all plugins and the CMS platform up to date?
Log in to your WordPress dashboard and check Dashboard → Updates. Outdated software is the most common entry point for website hacks. A site running outdated software is a question of when, not if, it will be compromised.
27
Is there a recent, tested backup of the website?
Ask your hosting provider or developer when the last backup was taken and whether it has been tested. A backup that cannot be successfully restored is worthless. Daily automated backups stored off-site are the minimum standard.

Chapter 8

When You Need a Professional

Some website issues look like maintenance tasks but are actually architectural problems. The following red flags indicate that DIY optimisation will not be sufficient — professional involvement is required.

  • Theme no longer supported — abandoned themes stop receiving security patches and will conflict with updated plugins over time
  • Reliance on outdated or abandoned plugins — removing them without replacements can break core site functionality
  • Page builder conflicts — multiple page builders used by different developers create bloated, conflicting code
  • PageSpeed remains below 50 after optimisation — the issue is architectural; a rebuild may be more cost-effective
  • Core Web Vitals failing despite optimisation — persistent failures indicate deep structural issues requiring a developer
  • Developer no longer contactable — you may not have access to your own hosting, domain, or CMS
  • More than two developers with no documentation — making changes in this environment carries significant risk of unintended consequences
Rule of thumb

If you have scored Fail on more than three technical items and cannot identify the cause, bring in a professional for a one-hour paid review before spending money on fixes. It will save time and money.

Chapter 10

The 90-Day Revenue Recovery Roadmap

Completing the checklist is the beginning, not the end. Use this roadmap to structure your improvement effort over the next 90 days.

Month 1 — Quick Wins
Focus exclusively on items in the Quick Win quadrant of the Priority Matrix — changes you can make today without a developer. Typical Month 1 actions: rewriting the homepage headline, adding a CTA above the fold, compressing all images, displaying Google reviews, adding a phone number to the header, removing unused plugins, and setting up uptime monitoring. None of these require technical expertise, and collectively they can produce a meaningful improvement in conversion rate within weeks.
Month 2 — Strategic Fixes
Tackle the structural and technical issues identified in the checklist. Address hosting performance, mobile responsiveness, contact form optimisation, and Google Analytics setup. Some items may require professional assistance — use the Red Flags section to determine which ones. If a rebuild is warranted, Month 2 is the time to begin briefing a developer.
Month 3 — Conversion Optimisation
With the foundation stabilised, shift focus to testing and refinement. Review your Google Analytics data from the previous two months. Which pages have the highest bounce rate? Which CTAs are being clicked? Where are visitors dropping off? Use this data to make targeted improvements to the pages and elements that matter most. Consider adding a case study section, updating testimonials, and reviewing the visitor journey on mobile.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

I am not technical — can I still use this guide?

Yes. Every diagnostic question is written in plain English. Where a tool is needed, it is named and the process described. You do not need to understand how websites are built to use this guide effectively.

What if I score very low — is my website beyond saving?

Not necessarily. A low score means your website has significant room for improvement — which is a positive finding. It means there are concrete, actionable changes that can meaningfully improve your results.

Do I need to hire someone to fix everything I identify?

No. Many items can be addressed independently — particularly in the areas of messaging, content, trust signals, and basic maintenance. The guide flags which issues typically require professional involvement.

How long will it take to complete the workbook?

Allow 90 to 120 minutes for both parts combined, including the competitor benchmarking worksheet. Working through it in one sitting produces the clearest picture.

How often should I run this diagnostic?

Annually, or after any significant change to your business, website, or market. A website performing well 18 months ago may have fallen behind as competitors improved their own sites or Google's ranking factors evolved.

I'm not sure what to do next after completing the workbook.

Bring your completed Health Score (slide 19) and Post-Workbook Action Plan to a conversation with a web professional. These documents give a developer immediate, specific insight — making the conversation far more productive.

Bonus 1 — Continued

The 7 Most Common Website Traps & DIY vs. Developer

Trap 1 — Locked into a proprietary platform

Some developers build on platforms they control. If you want to change developers, your website may not be transferable. Always confirm the site can be moved.

Trap 2 — Site never properly handed over

Many business owners discover — when something goes wrong — that they do not have access to their own hosting, domain, or CMS. Insist on receiving all credentials at project completion.

Trap 3 — Template disguised as custom design

A template is not inherently bad — but it should be priced accordingly and disclosed upfront. Paying $8,000 for a lightly modified $60 theme is not what you're paying for.

Trap 4 — No staging environment

Developers who make changes directly to your live website are one mistake away from taking it offline. A staging environment is standard professional practice.

Trap 5 — Maintenance fees, no maintenance performed

Some developers charge monthly fees for backups never taken, updates never applied, security never monitored. Ask for a monthly report confirming work performed.

Trap 6 — Contracts with no clear deliverables

Vague contracts give you no recourse if the result doesn't meet expectations. Insist on a detailed scope listing every page, feature, and deliverable with milestones.

Trap 7 — Choosing a developer based on price alone

The cheapest quote is rarely the best value. A website built poorly costs more to fix than to build correctly — and costs you leads every day in between.
